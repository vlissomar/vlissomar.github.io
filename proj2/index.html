Fun with Filters and Frequencies

 This project focuses on image filtering, from the how-to, like convolution, to applications, like extracting and manipulate low and high frequencies in images, and evventually image blending.

01   Filters and Edges

1.1: Convolutions from Scratch!

 For both 2D convolution implementations, I preprocess by flipping the kernel and finding a ‘center’ pixel location. Then, I iterate over the output matrix to fill in values.

 Boundaries are handled by padding with zero fill values, as specified by the specs. In the 4 for-loop version, any pixel that would be in the padding zone is simply ignored as out of bounds, while in the 2 for-loop version, the matrix slice includes an actual padding of zeroes.
 Regarding runtime, the 4 for-loop version is the one with the worse performance, taking seconds each call. On the other hand, the 2 for-loop version and scipy implementations spend less than a tenth of a second, with the scipy almost halving the 2 for-loop implementation. The lines of ‘True True True’ are from checks with np.allclose(...) comparing the results of convolution. scipy’s convolve2d uses mode=‘same’ and has zero padding by default.

1.2: Finite Difference Operator

 Each of the finite difference filters essentially computing partial derivatives, and make up the gradient. To find the magnitude of the gradient, we take the square_root(sum(squared(partial derivatives))).
 After binarizing the gradient magnitude, I personally preferred the threshold of 0.2 (assuming the image ranges from 0:black to 1:white. Lower thresholds reveal more building detail, but leaves the grass looking very noisy. Higher thresholds make the edge lines appear to disconnect sometimes, which is also undesirable.

1.3: Derivative of Gaussian (DoG) Filter

 The derivative of Gaussian filters can be quickly found by convolving the Gaussian with the D_x and D_y filters.

(I * G) * D_xy vs I * (G * D_xy)

 As can be seen, applying two kernel convolutions has the same effect as convolving the two kernels first, then applying the new kernel to the image. The latter is simply more efficient, because kernels tend to be smaller, so convolution takes less computations.
 Additionally, compared to the previous part, the remaining amount of noise (in the grass) is completely removed by blurring first.

02   Applications

2.1: Image "Sharpening"

  A blur filter is a low-pass filter that removes high frequencies. When subtracting blur(img) from img, only high frequencies, details, remain. If these high frequencies are once again overlain on top of the image, a ‘sharpening’ effect is made.
 As can be seen, alpha is proportional to the sharpening. As alpha becomes too large, however, there seems to be a bit of overexposure-like effect, especially when examining the small rocky surfaces on the right side. At lower alpha, these matte highlights of the yellow rock/wall are still yellow, but at higher alpha, they look closer to white. This may be due to implementation, as currently I clip values that exceed the desired range, as opposed to rescaling the image.

2.2: Hybrid Images

Behold!
 As directed in the specs, first, the images need to be aligned. Then, I would remove the high frequencies from the first image, and the low frequencies from the other. As earlier, extracting low frequencies uses a Gaussian blur, and extracting high frequencies comes from subtracting away low frequencies. Combining the filtered images, we can get the hybrid images.

Walkthrough!

 It’s interesting to see how the image alignment affects the FFTs. The panda gets rotated, and that can be seen in the FFT. I suspect the vertical/horizontal lines are due to the border of the image, so when the image is zero filled upon rotating, there still remains a sharp edge that the FFT copies and rotates with it. It’s more difficult to see how FFT changes for the tiger, which gets a zoom out. The corresponding effect in the Fourier domain should be a zoom in, but because of a lack of distinctive points on the Fourier, I can’t quite verify that is the case.
 During the filter stage, the FFT clearly drops high frequencies for the low-pass, which appears as a white cross. On the other hand, the high-pass filtered image’s region of blocked frequencies appears to be just a pixel in size, at the very center.
 The hybrid combines both sets of frequencies, so the FFT is not that distinctive when compared to other images with low and high frequencies, like that of the unaligned input images.

2.3: Gaussian and Laplacian Stacks

 Gaussian and Laplacian stacks are actually quite straightforward, especially since there's no down/upsampling involved like in pyramids. The difference between each level of Gaussian is a power of two on the σ used to blur them. G(0) would be blended with σ = 2^0 = 1, G(1) has σ = 2^1 = 2, and so on. Laplacians are even easier. L(i) = G(i) - G(i + 1), except for the last one, for which L(i) = G(i).

2.4: Multiresolution Blending (A.K.A. The Oraple!)

 Now that we've acquired our Laplacian stacks, we can use them to smoothly blend together different images. In order to do so, a mask indicating the seam where the two images will be spliced together is needed. On the mask, we compute a Gaussian stack, and use the values as weights when blending. Essentially, we have L_blend[i] = L_A[i] * (1 - M[i]) + L_B[i] * M[i], where i is the level in the stack, M is the mask, and L_A, L_B are the Laplacian stacks of image A and image B. The * symbol only means elementwise multiplication, not convolution. Lastly, we find the resulting blended image summing all levels for L_blend.
