Part A: Exploring the Power of Flow Models

01  Visualizing Training and Sampling

1.1: Constructing Noisy Training Inputs

 The noisy training inputs are simply an interpolation between the clean image and a noise image sampled from the Gaussian. Interpolate with parameter t, such that t=0 is pure noise, and t=1 is the clean image.

1.2: Classical Denoising

 Here, we try to denoise the image by simply blurring out the noise. As shown below, the necessary Gaussian blur sigma parameter increases with the amount of noise in the image.

1.3: One-Step Denoising

  With the Gaussian blur as a baseline, we now examine the results from using a Flow-Matching model to denoise in one step. Smaller t have a larger step to make, and the results are similarly more smooth. Fine details must have been lost with high noise.

1.4: Visualizing Velocity

 The predicted velocity is set to be compared with the ground truth. As shown below, the noised images with the larger t to step have a more vibrant error. It indicates that the result of the one-step denoising will be less similar to the truth than noised images with smaller t.

1.5: Iterative Denoising with Euler Sampling

  Euler denoising with small steps of 0.02 bring us to a remarkably clean image at t=1.0. At each step, we predict a velocity, then add it to out current x_t, scaled by h to get x_t+h. Evidently, Euler sampling performs the best out of our three denoising methods.

1.1-2: Implementing the UNet & Using the UNet to Train a Denoiser

  In this section, we set up the UNet, defining each operation (simple and composed), and using them to construct our layers following the flowchart in the specs. Then, we conduct self-supervised learning, passing in noised images as input. Below are examples of what our noised images should look like, for different σ that are used to interpolate between clean images and noise.
  After the model is trained, we can examine the loss at each optimization step, as well as the results of our denoiser for a specific value of σ=0.5.

1.2.2: Out-of-Distribution Testing

  We can also test our model on σ values outside of its distribution. For the most part, performance appears decent up to σ=0.6.

1.2.3: Denoising Pure Noise

  Now, we try training another model on pure noise. Generally, each denoised image result looks the exact same, as though all digits are superimposed on each other, regardless of the target clean image. If like in the normal scenario (train the model like in 1.2.1), the model sees a noisy image, there will still be features present in the noisy images that hint as to what to do, allowing it to make better predictions. In this scenario, the model never sees anything related to the true image it was trying to predict. As a result, the best prediction the model can make under the MSE error is just the E[x], the average of all the images.

02  Training a Flow Matching Model

2.1-3: UNet Time Conditioning: Training & Sampling

  Similar to in Part 1, we again define some operators that will be used in our UNet to incorporate time conditioning. This time, the model recieves both noisy image x_t as well as t, and is optimized with the MSE between true and predicted velocities. After it's trained, we sample Gaussian noise and perform flow matching to generate images of the digits. While the results at all appear to at least be some kind of hand-scrawled symbol, only with higher number of epochs does the writing actually begin to resemble handwritten digits.

2.4-6: UNet Class Conditioning: Training & Sampling

  Now, we add class-conditioning into the mix. With class conditioning, the all images samples evidently appear as digits, even with only one epoch. Not only can we control what digit the flow matching will output, we also get better performance.
